Salaries Tax — Worked Illustrations

Every worked example backing the Salaries Tax page — drawn from IRD's DIPNs and from Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.). Figures are cross-checked against current YA 2026/27 allowances/rates; any outdated textbook figure is corrected and flagged.

A. From IRD DIPNs

Illustration — Joint assessment saves tax vs separate taxation DIPN 18

Mr A earns $680,000, Mrs A earns $100,000. Taxed separately, their combined liability is $23,030. Electing joint assessment under s.10(2) — pooling income and allowances — reduces the combined liability to $17,505, because Mrs A's unabsorbed allowances/deductions offset Mr A's higher-rate income.

Illustration — Home loan interest apportionment by ownership type DIPN 35

Mr C is sole owner, pays $120,000 interest — allowable deduction capped at $100,000. Mr & Mrs D are joint tenants, pay $100,000 total interest between them — each gets $50,000. Mr E (1/4 share) and Mr F (3/4 share) are tenants in common on a $100,000 interest bill — Mr E gets $25,000, Mr F gets $75,000 (apportioned strictly by ownership share, not by who actually paid).

Illustration — VHIS premium cap applied per insured person DIPN 56

Mr J pays VHIS premiums for himself ($5,000), spouse ($4,000), grandmother ($16,000), father ($12,000), mother ($11,000), son ($3,000) — total $51,000 paid. Because the cap is HKD 8,000 per insured person, the grandmother/father/mother claims are each capped at $8,000: allowable deduction = $5,000 + $4,000 + ($8,000 × 3) + $3,000 = $36,000. The $15,000 excess simply lapses — no carry-forward.

Illustration — Charitable donation cap at 35% of reduced assessable income DIPN 37

Mr A has salary $500,000, deductible expenses $10,000 (reduced assessable income $490,000), and made donations of $210,000. Since $210,000 exceeds 35% of $490,000, the deduction is capped at $490,000 × 35% = $171,500 — the remaining $38,500 of donations gives no further tax benefit.

Illustration — Share option gain apportioned by vesting days in Hong Kong DIPN 38

An employee's share option has a 730-day vesting period, of which 292 days were spent working in Hong Kong. At exercise, only 292/730 = 40% of the gain is assessable to Hong Kong salaries tax — the remainder relates to non-Hong Kong employment and falls outside the charge.

B. From Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.)

Adapted from a professional taxation qualification manual. Facts are paraphrased, not reproduced verbatim; every allowance/rate figure has been checked against current Hong Kong tax law and corrected where the textbook was using a stale amount (noted per illustration).

Illustration — The 60-day rule: two early departures tip the count over the line Module 9, Ch.4 §4.1 — s.8(1B)

An employee holds a Hong Kong employment contract but works full-time in Mainland China, returning to Hong Kong only periodically. His visits (quarterly meetings plus connecting travel) total 59 "ordinary" days for the year. On two occasions, however, he leaves Hong Kong very early in the morning to connect onward (once via Osaka, once via London) rather than flying straight back to China. Under the counting convention, both the day of arrival and the day of departure count as a full day each — even for a same-day transit.

Days in HK from ordinary visits59
Extra day — early departure via Osaka+1
Extra day — early departure via London+1
Total days in Hong Kong61

61 > 60, so the s.8(1B) exemption fails entirely — his full Hong Kong-employment income becomes chargeable, even though he worked in China almost all year. It's a hard cliff-edge test on days present, not days worked, and transit days count in full. No allowance/rate figures involved, so no correction was needed.

Illustration — Rent-free housing benefit: the "rental value" method Module 9, Ch.4 §4.2 — s.9(2)

An employee on a $600,000 salary gets a company-leased apartment rent-free; the employer also reimburses $16,800 of medical insurance (a taxable perquisite). Rental value = 10% of (salary + taxable perquisites) for a self-contained flat/house.

Salary + taxable perquisite$616,800
Rental value (10% × $616,800)$61,680

Contrast — two staff sharing one room in a company flat (boarding-house-type arrangement, not a self-contained flat) use the lower 4% rate instead of 10%:

EmployeeIncomeRental value (4%)
Staff A$150,000$6,000
Staff B$120,000$4,800

Rate scale (s.9(2)): flat/house = 10% of net employment income; hotel/hostel/boarding-house of ≤2 rooms = 8%; ≤1 room / shared-room = 4%. These percentages sit in the Ordinance itself (not an annually-adjusted allowance) — no update needed, but worth a one-line confirmation against IRD's current housing-benefit guide before relying on it for a filing.

Illustration — Share option gain: OMV at exercise, less strike price and option cost Module 9, Ch.4 §4.3 — s.9(1)(d)/(4)

An employee is granted an option (by virtue of employment) to buy 5,000 shares at a strike price of $12/share, paying $6,000 for the option itself. Open market value (OMV) at grant was $24/share; two years later he exercises when OMV has risen to $30/share.

OMV at exercise (5,000 × $30)$150,000
Less: consideration paid on exercise (5,000 × $12)($60,000)
Less: cost of the option itself($6,000)
Chargeable gain, assessed in year of exercise$84,000

The gain is taxed at exercise, not at eventual sale — any share-price movement after exercise is a capital (non-taxable) matter. No allowance/rate figures involved, so no correction was needed.

Illustration — Termination gratuity "related back" over a 36-month window Module 9, Ch.4 §4.6 — s.11D(b)(i)

An employee made redundant after 7 years 11 months of service receives a $140,400 contract-end gratuity and elects to have it "related back" under s.11D(b)(i). Because total service exceeded 3 years, the law spreads the gratuity evenly over the 36 months ending on the earlier of the payment date or last day of employment — not over the full 7-year service period.

Year of assessmentMonths in the 36-month windowGratuity allocated
Year 1 (partial)8$31,200
Year 212$46,800
Year 312$46,800
Year 4 (partial)4$15,600
Total36$140,400

Contrast: for service of 3 years or less, the gratuity spreads only over the actual contract/service period, not artificially capped at 36 months. Pure time-apportionment mechanic — no correction needed.

Illustration — Child allowance depends on "full-time education," not just age Module 9, Ch.4 §4.9 — s.31

Twins both turn 19 partway through a year and finish secondary school that same year — the allowance still applies for that year since both were in full-time education for part of it (age >18 doesn't disqualify a student). The following year, one twin enrols in a full-time vocational diploma (still qualifies); the other instead starts a technician apprenticeship structured as an employment contract — drawing a wage as an employee rather than being a student — which fails the "full-time education" condition.

YearChildren qualifyingChild allowance claimable (current YA 2026/27 rate: $140,000/child)
Year twins turn 19Both twins2 × $140,000 = $280,000
Following yearDiploma student only1 × $140,000 = $140,000

Original book figure used $100,000/child (correct for YA 2018/19, now stale); updated above to the current YA 2026/27 rate of $140,000/child. Mechanic: no age cap while a child remains in full-time education; a paid apprenticeship/employment contract breaks eligibility even though the person is still "training."

Illustration — Full computation: salary, housing benefit, share option gain, deductions, allowances, tax payable Module 9, Ch.4 §4.11 — the "master" illustration

A bank compliance director receives salary $1,500,000, bonus $200,000, a rent-free company flat (taxed via the 10% rental-value method), and a gain on exercising vested overseas stock options. He deducts a professional subscription, self-education fees, a charitable donation, and mandatory MPF, and claims the basic and child allowances (married, assessed as an individual).

Note: a one-off percentage tax reduction (capped at a fixed HKD amount) is announced in most Budgets and would further reduce this figure for the specific year being computed — check the current year's Budget measure before treating $230,893 as the final "net tax payable" (see the one-off relief callout on the main Salaries Tax page).

C. Personal Assessment — from Module 9 (Textbook)

Personal Assessment (§6 of the main page) aggregates salaries, property, and unincorporated-business income into one computation. These three illustrations, adapted from Module 9's running "Chan/Lip family" case study, are recomputed against current allowances/rate bands throughout.

Illustration — Should a sole proprietor with rental property elect Personal Assessment? Module 9, Ch.6 §6.3 — s.41

A taxpayer runs a sole-proprietorship business (profit $800,000, of which he donates $400,000 to charity) and separately owns a rental property (rental income $400,000), while paying $400,000 mortgage interest on his own home. Without election, profits tax and property tax are assessed separately with no relief for the home loan interest at all, and the charitable donation is capped at 35% of the business profit alone.

ItemWithout electionWith Personal Assessment
Business profit, after donation capped at 35% of $800,000 ($280,000 used, $120,000 of the $400,000 paid gets no relief)Profits tax @15% on $520,000 = $78,000Combined income $840,000, less a recomputed donation ceiling (35% of grossed-up income — $112,000 more becomes usable), less home loan interest (capped at $100,000/yr), less married allowance ($290,000) → net chargeable income $338,000 → tax at progressive rates = $39,460
Rental property, NAV after 20% allowanceProperty tax @15% on $320,000 = $48,000
Total$126,000
Tax saved by electing$86,540

Original book figures used the 2017/18 married allowance ($264,000) and old 4-band rates; updated above to current allowances/bands. Key trap: under Personal Assessment, the charitable donation ceiling is recomputed as 35% of the grossed-up combined income, not simply 35% of the post-donation business profit — this typically unlocks extra donation relief. Home loan interest also becomes claimable for the first time once there is a combined computation to set it against, even though the taxpayer has no salaries income. Figures exclude any one-off Budget tax reduction for the specific year being computed — check the current year's measure before treating either total as final.

Illustration — Married couple, joint election: salaries + property + business + partnership loss Module 9, Ch.6 §6.4 — s.41(1A)

Husband has salaries income $520,000 plus a $120,000 share of a partnership loss. Wife owns a rental property (NAV $192,000, mortgage interest ≥ $192,000) and runs a business (net profit $400,000 after an approved donation already used at business level). One 15-year-old child.

Husband's income − partnership loss share$520,000 − $120,000 = $400,000
Wife's income − mortgage interest (capped at NAV) − extra donation relief$692,000 − $192,000 − $160,000 = $340,000
Combined reduced total income$740,000
Less: married allowance (current rate)($290,000)
Less: child allowance × 1 (current rate)($140,000)
Net chargeable income$310,000
Tax at progressive rates$34,700

Tax is then apportioned back to each spouse in proportion to their own net contribution to the combined income: husband $400,000/$740,000 × $34,700 ≈ $18,757; wife $340,000/$740,000 × $34,700 ≈ $15,943.

Original book figures used the 2017/18 married allowance ($264,000) and child allowance ($100,000); updated above to current rates ($290,000 / $140,000). Mechanic: aggregating income across both spouses, capping loan interest at the property's own NAV (not the higher amount actually paid), and apportioning the joint tax bill back to each spouse by their share of net income — not a 50/50 split.

Illustration — Dependent parent allowance, elderly care deduction, and a business loss driving one spouse's share to zero Module 9, Ch.6 §6.5 — ss.26D, 31

Husband has employment income (incl. a share option gain), pays for his elderly mother's support (not living with him) and his elderly father's residential care home fees. Wife has no employment, runs a business at a loss for the year, and owns a rental property with mortgage interest exceeding its NAV.

Husband's net assessable income + Wife's NAV$1,368,000 + $105,600 = $1,473,600
Less: mortgage interest (capped at wife's NAV)($105,600)
Less: approved donation, MPF (capped)($10,000 + $18,000)

Original book figures used 2017/18 rates throughout (married allowance $264,000, dependent parent allowance $46,000, elderly care cap $92,000); all updated above to current rates. Because the wife's own income is more than wiped out by her mortgage interest, donation, and business loss, her apportioned share of the joint tax floors at nil — the husband bears the entire $54,250. Note the dependent parent allowance (mother, not co-residing) and the elderly care deduction (father, in a care home) apply to different dependants — you cannot double-claim both reliefs for the same parent, but can use different reliefs for different parents in the same year.

Figures to re-verify before publishing The elderly residential care expenses cap and any one-off percentage tax reduction (with its HKD cap) are announced fresh in most annual Budgets — confirm both against the current year's Budget measure before treating any "final tax payable" figure above as authoritative. The MPF mandatory-contribution cap ($18,000) and 35%-of-income donation ceiling are carried through from established practice; a quick check against current IRD guidance is still worthwhile before publishing.
