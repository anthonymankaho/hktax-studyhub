Profits Tax — Worked Illustrations

Every worked example backing the Profits Tax page, in one place — drawn from IRD's DIPNs and from Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.). Figures are cross-checked against current law; any outdated textbook figure is corrected and flagged.

A. From IRD DIPNs

Illustration — Lease premium: Profits Tax vs Property Tax interaction DIPN 4

Company H grants a 6-year lease of its property for a premium of $1,200,000, paid up front. As a person carrying on a property-dealing/letting business, the premium is a revenue receipt taxable under Profits Tax — s.15C spreads it evenly over the lease term, so 1/6 = $200,000 is assessed each year for 6 years. Separately, s.5B(4) deems the premium payable in equal monthly instalments for Property Tax purposes but capped at 3 years, so 1/3 = $400,000 is assessed for Property Tax in each of Years 1–3 only.

YearProperty Tax paid
($400,000 × 80% × 16%)		Profits Tax
($200,000 × 17.5%)		Net position
1–3 (each)$51,200$35,000Refund of $16,200 (Property Tax set off against Profits Tax under s.25)
4–6 (each)—$35,000$35,000 payable

Grand total tax over 6 years = $210,000, i.e. exactly 17.5% of the $1,200,000 premium (the historical standard rate used in the DIPN's example year) — the set-off mechanism ensures the premium is not taxed twice under both heads.

Illustration — Interest deduction restricted by a tax-free secured deposit DIPN 13A

Taxpayer F borrows $1,000,000 at 5% interest, secured by a $1,000,000 deposit earning tax-exempt interest at 4%. Because the loan is secured on a deposit generating tax-free income, s.16(2A)/(2B) restrict the deduction: the $40,000 tax-free interest earned reduces the otherwise-deductible $50,000 loan interest, leaving only $10,000 deductible.

Illustration — Service company "Type II" management fee cap DIPN 24

A professional firm pays $4,000,000 in management fees to a related service company whose actual attributable cost of providing those services is $3,000,000. The deduction is capped at cost plus a 12.5% mark-up: $3,000,000 × 112.5% = $3,375,000. The $625,000 excess is added back, increasing assessable profits from $1,000,000 (per accounts) to $1,625,000.

Illustration — Foreign tax deductible only where no DTA credit is available DIPN 28

Bank-HK pays income tax on interest income in Jurisdiction F. If Hong Kong has no double tax agreement with Jurisdiction F, the foreign tax is deductible under s.16(1)(c). If Hong Kong does have a DTA with Jurisdiction F, s.16(2J) denies the deduction — Bank-HK must instead claim a tax credit under s.50, since double relief (deduction plus credit) is not permitted.

Illustration — Hong Kong bank deposit interest exemption straddling the exemption date DIPN 34

A taxpayer has $10,000,000 trading profits plus $4,000,000 of local bank deposit interest, of which $1,000,000 accrued before 22 June 1998 (the exemption's effective date) and $3,000,000 accrued after. Only the pre-exemption $1,000,000 is taxable; total 1998/99 assessable profits = $11,000,000 rather than $14,000,000.

Illustration — Prepaid expenses follow accounting treatment, not cash timing DIPN 40, following CIR v Secan

Following the Secan case, a full cash prepayment (e.g. 3 years' rent or insurance paid up front) is no longer deductible in full in the year of payment if the applicable accounting standard requires it to be amortised over the period it relates to — the tax deduction now follows the accounting/matching treatment, reversing the Department's older cash-basis practice.

B. From Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.)

Adapted from a professional taxation qualification manual. Facts are paraphrased, not reproduced verbatim; every figure has been checked against current Hong Kong tax law and corrected where the textbook is outdated (noted per illustration).

Illustration — Basis period on commencement: first accounts cover 18 months Module 9, Ch.3 §3.9.2 — s.18C(1)(b)

A company commences business on 1 July 2018 and adopts a 31 December accounting date, but its first set of accounts is not closed until 31 December 2019 — an 18-month period. Adjusted profits: $90,000 for the 18 months to 31 Dec 2019, then $68,000 for the 12 months to 31 Dec 2020.

Where the first accounting period exceeds 12 months, s.18C(1)(b) gives the Commissioner discretion to take the second year of assessment as the 12 months counted back from the end of the first accounting period, with the commencement year taking the remaining stub — both by time-apportioning the 18-month profit.

Year of assessmentBasis periodAssessable profitsBasis
Commencement year1/7/2018 – 31/12/2018 (6 mo.)$30,000 (= $90,000 × 6/18)s.18C(1)(b)
Following year1/1/2019 – 31/12/2019 (12 mo.)$60,000 (= $90,000 × 12/18)s.18B(2)
Third year1/1/2020 – 31/12/2020 (12 mo.)$68,000s.18B(2)

Check: $30,000 + $60,000 = $90,000 — the full 18-month profit is accounted for exactly once. No tax rate is involved in this mechanic, so no currency correction was needed.

Illustration — Retirement scheme contribution cap (s.17(1)(h)) Module 9, Ch.3 §3.5.4

An employee earns an annual salary of $100,000. The employer makes an ordinary annual contribution to a recognised retirement scheme equal to 20% of salary, i.e. $20,000.

s.17(1)(h) disallows the deduction of ordinary retirement-scheme contributions to the extent they exceed 15% of the employee's total emoluments: cap = 15% × $100,000 = $15,000 deductible; the remaining $5,000 is non-deductible.

The 15% cap is unchanged in current law — no correction needed.

Illustration — Loss relief: carry-forward vs. surrender on change of trade Module 9, Ch.3 §3.11 — s.19C

Part A — corporation, multi-year carry-forward: a company realises losses of $25M (2016/17) and $75M (2017/18), giving $100M carried forward. It then realises a profit of $80M in 2018/19.

Part B — sole proprietor, same trade vs. changed trade: a sole proprietor has an unrelieved loss of $1,000,000 brought forward from a wine-distribution trade.

ScenarioResult
Continues the same wine trade, earning $1,200,000 in the current year$1,200,000 − $1,000,000 loss b/f = $200,000 taxable
Ceases the wine trade and starts an unrelated fruit-trading business, earning $500,000Loss is tied to the ceased trade and is surrendered — full $500,000 taxable, no relief

Losses only carry forward against future profits of the same source (s.19C) — changing to an unrelated trade forfeits the loss. No tax rate is involved in either part, so no correction was needed.

Illustration — Secured loan test: interest deduction restricted by a tax-free deposit Module 9, Ch.3 §3.5.3.8 — s.16(2A), adapted from DIPN 13A

A taxpayer borrows $1,000,000 from a bank at 5% p.a. ($50,000 interest) for general business use, secured by an overseas deposit earning tax-free interest. The allowable deduction depends on how the security is structured:

CaseSecurityTax-free interest earnedDeduction reduced byInterest allowed
1Deposit $1,000,000 only$40,000$40,000 (full match)$10,000
2Deposit $500,000 + shares $500,000$20,000 (deposit only)$20,000 (shares ignored — not a deposit)$30,000
3Deposit $2,000,000 (exceeds loan)$80,000Pro-rated: $1,000,000/$2,000,000 × $80,000 = $40,000$10,000
4Deposit $1,000,000 + shares $500,000$40,000 (deposit only)Pro-rated by security mix: $1,000,000/$1,500,000 × $40,000 = $26,667$23,333

Only deposits/loans generating tax-free interest trigger this restriction — other security (shares, property, guarantees) is ignored for this test. No profits tax rate is involved, so no correction was needed.

Illustration — Interest flow-back test: debenture interest paid to an untaxed associate Module 9, Ch.3 §3.5.3.8 — s.16(2C), adapted from DIPN 13A

A Hong Kong company issues $10,000,000 of debentures to fund its HK trading business, paying $1,000,000 total interest for the year. $8,000,000 of the debentures is held all year by its overseas subsidiary, an associate not taxed in Hong Kong on the interest it receives.

Proportion held by the untaxed associate$8,000,000 / $10,000,000 = 8/10
Interest disallowed (flows back untaxed)$1,000,000 × 8/10 = $800,000
Allowable interest deduction$200,000

If the associate held the debentures for only 6 of the 12 months, the disallowance is further time-apportioned: $800,000 × 6/12 = $400,000 disallowed instead.

Exception: no restriction applies if the associate is a market maker holding the debentures in the ordinary course of its business (s.16(2G)). No profits tax rate is involved, so no correction was needed.
