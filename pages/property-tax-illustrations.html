Property Tax — Worked Illustrations

Every worked example backing the Property Tax page — drawn from IRD's DIPNs and from Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.). The 15% standard rate and 20% statutory allowance are unchanged in current law, so these textbook figures needed no correction.

A. From IRD DIPNs

Illustration — Premium spread over the statutory 3-year cap DIPN 14

A $60,000 premium is paid for a 5-year lease running from 1 June 2006 to 31 May 2011 (60 months). Because s.5B(4) caps the spreading period at 3 years (36 months) even though the lease itself runs 5 years, the premium is spread over 36 months only:

Year of assessmentMonths falling in yearAssessable value included
2006/0710 of 36$16,667
2007/0812 of 36$20,000
2008/0912 of 36$20,000
2009/102 of 36$3,333

Nothing is assessed for 2010/11 or 2011/12 even though the lease itself continues — the 3-year statutory cap has already fully absorbed the premium.

Illustration — Net assessable value computation chain DIPN 14

The DIPN sets out the standard computation chain: Consideration receivable, plus rent recovered previously written off, less irrecoverable rent, gives Assessable Value; deduct rates paid by the owner (if any); deduct the flat 20% statutory allowance on the balance, to arrive at Net Assessable Value — the figure to which the 15% standard rate is applied.

Illustration — Premium taxed twice under two different spreading rules DIPN 4 (see also Profits Tax page)

Where the property owner is also carrying on a property-dealing/letting trade, the same premium is spread differently for the two taxes: over the full lease term (no cap) for Profits Tax purposes as a trading receipt, but capped at 3 years for Property Tax purposes under s.5B(4). The s.25 set-off then reconciles the two — see the full worked example on the Profits Tax illustrations page.

B. From Module 9: Principles of Taxation (4th ed.)

Adapted from a professional taxation qualification manual, following its running "Tong family" case study. Facts are paraphrased, not reproduced verbatim. The 15% standard rate and 20% statutory allowance used throughout already match current law — no correction was needed for this topic.

Illustration — Premium spreading: lease term shorter than, equal to, and longer than the 3-year cap Module 9, Ch.5 §5.2 — s.5B(3)/(4)

Three properties are let on different lease terms, each with a lump-sum premium spread evenly over the lease period or 3 years, whichever is shorter:

PropertyLease termPremiumSpreading
A5 years (60 months)$360,000Capped at 36 months → $10,000/month for 3 years, then nil for the remaining 2 years
B3 years (36 months, exactly)$240,000Full term used (36 = cap) → $6,667/month throughout
C2 years (24 months)$144,000No cap needed → $6,000/month throughout

Property A shows the cap actually biting: the $360,000 premium is fully exhausted at month 36, and nothing further is assessed for the lease's remaining 24 months even though the tenancy continues.

Three commonly-tested variations on the NAV computation chain (all at the current 15%/20% rates):

Key traps illustrated: (1) rates are only deductible if the owner agreed to bear them — Property B's tenant pays rates, so no deduction; (2) government rent, management fees, mortgage interest, and repair costs actually paid by the owner are never separately deductible — the flat 20% allowance is deemed to cover all of them regardless of actual spend.

Illustration — Irrecoverable rent exceeding the year's own rent, carried back Module 9, Ch.5 §5.4 — s.7C

A 2-year tenancy at $10,000/month runs from 1 July 2016; the tenant stops paying from 1 January 2017 and disappears on 1 May 2017, with the assessor accepting the outstanding rent as bad.

Original YA1 (filed before default known, 9 months' rent)YA2 (year debt confirmed bad)
Rent / assessable value$90,000$10,000
Less: bad debt confirmed irrecoverable (4 months, Jan–Apr)—($40,000)
Result for YA2—($30,000) — a loss

Since the $40,000 bad debt exceeds YA2's own $10,000 rent, the $30,000 excess is carried back under s.7C(3) to amend YA1:

YA1 revised: $90,000 rent − $30,000 carried-back loss$60,000
Less: 20% statutory allowance($12,000)
Revised NAV$48,000
Revised tax @15% ($7,200) vs. originally paid ($10,800)Refund of $3,600

Mere non-payment (without the debt being proved bad) gives no deduction at all — this only applies once the assessor accepts the rent as genuinely irrecoverable.

Illustration — Property tax set off against profits tax for a corporation Module 9, Ch.5 §5.6 — s.25

A company's rental income is already included in its accounts and taxed under profits tax. It did not apply for the s.5(2)(a) upfront exemption, and separately paid $3,000 property tax on the same property.

Profits tax payable for the year$10,000
Less: property tax already paid on the same property($3,000)
Net profits tax payable$7,000

A straight set-off, unaffected by rate levels — if property tax paid had exceeded the profits tax bill, the excess would be refundable instead.
